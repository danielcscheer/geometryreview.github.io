A

Parts of a Sail

  • Name the three edges and three corners of a sail
  • Explain what telltales, battens, and roach do
A mainsail: three edges (luff, leech, foot), three corners (head, tack, clew), plus battens, roach, and telltales.
Edges & corners
Luff
Forward edge — runs along the mast.
Leech
Trailing (back) edge — where airflow exits.
Foot
Bottom edge — runs along the boom.
Head
Top corner — hoisted by the halyard.
Tack
Bottom-forward corner — fixed at the mast/boom.
Clew
Bottom-aft corner — where the sheet attaches.

Telltales are short ribbons on the sail that show airflow — you trim the sail until they stream straight back on both sides. Battens are stiff slats in pockets along the leech that support roach, the extra sail area that bulges past a straight line from head to clew.

Example
You glance up and the windward (outside) telltale is streaming straight back, but the leeward (inside) telltale is fluttering and stalling.
1
A stalled leeward telltale usually means the sail is trimmed in too far, or you're pointed too close to the wind for the current trim.
2
Fix it by easing the sheet slightly, or by falling off a few degrees, until both telltales stream evenly.
Even, straight telltales on both sides = clean, attached airflow = an efficiently trimmed sail.
B

Parts of the Boat

  • Label the hull, spars, appendages, and steering gear
  • Separate standing rigging (fixed) from running rigging (adjustable)
Top-down hull: bow (front), stern (back), midship (center), cockpit, and beam (the boat's width at its widest point).
Standing rigging (fixed wires that hold the mast up): forestay, shrouds, backstay, spreaders.
Full parts list
Hull
Stern, cockpit, midship, beam, deck, bow.
Spars
Boom, mast, spar (general term for a pole), masthead fly (wind vane), painter (bow line), bridle (load-spreading line).
Underwater foils
Daggerboard (slides straight down), centerboard (pivots up), keel (fixed, ballasted) — all resist sideways slipping.
Steering
Tiller (the arm you hold), rudder (the underwater blade), pintles (pins on the rudder), gudgeons (sockets on the hull the pintles drop into).
Standing rigging
Forestay, shrouds, backstay, mast, spreaders — fixed, holds the mast up.
Running rigging
Mainsheet, halyard, jib sheet, Cunningham, outhaul, leech line — adjustable, controls sail shape.

Easy test: if you can adjust it while sailing to change sail shape or trim, it's running rigging. If it's a fixed wire holding the mast in place, it's standing rigging.

Example
Match the job to the line: "raises the mainsail," "controls how far out the boom swings," "tensions the luff of the mainsail," "tensions the foot along the boom."
1
Raises the sail → halyard.
2
Controls boom angle → mainsheet.
3
Tensions the luff → Cunningham.
4
Tensions the foot → outhaul.
C

Different Types of Boat

  • Tell a catboat from a sloop
  • Recognize crab claw, Bermuda, lateen, and square rigs
Catboat: one mast set well forward, one sail, no jib. Sloop: one mast set further aft, mainsail + jib — the most common recreational rig.
Four historic rig/sail shapes.
Rig types, in a nutshell
Crab claw
Two curved spars forming a crescent; ancient Pacific Islander design, surprisingly efficient.
Bermuda (Marconi)
Tall triangular sail; what nearly every modern sailboat uses; best upwind performance.
Lateen
Triangular sail on a long angled yard; classic Mediterranean/Arab dhow rig; better upwind than square rig.
Square rig
Rectangular sails on horizontal yards (tall ships); powerful downwind, poor upwind.

Upwind ability, worst to best: square < lateen/crab claw < Bermuda.

Example
You see a small dinghy with one mast set near the bow and a single large sail — no headsail. What is it, and how does that compare to a boat with a jib?
1
One mast, one sail, no jib = a catboat.
2
A sloop (main + jib) has more sail controls to learn, but the jib helps balance the boat and adds power/pointing ability via the slot effect (see Section K).
D

Direction

  • Fore/aft, amidships, port/starboard
  • Windward/leeward, heading up/falling off
Fore = toward the bow, aft = toward the stern, amidships = the middle. Facing forward, port is your left, starboard is your right.
Key terms
Windward
The side / direction the wind is coming from.
Leeward
The side / direction the wind is blowing toward.
Heading up
Turning the bow closer toward the wind.
Falling off
Turning the bow away from the wind.
Port & Left both = 4 lettersPort wine is red → port light is redStarboard light is green
Example
You're sailing close-hauled with the wind hitting your left (port) side. A gust hits and the boat starts to heel too much. What do you do with the boat's heading?
1
Head up slightly (turn toward the wind) to spill some power out of the sails, or ease the sheet.
2
Since the wind is on your port side, your port side is the windward side and your starboard side is leeward.
E

Knots

  • Know the purpose of a stopper knot, a bowline, and a cleat hitch
Schematic loop patterns — practice the real rope moves with your instructor.
What each knot is for
Stopper (figure-8)
Tied at the end of a line so it can't run out through a block or your hand.
Bowline
Makes a fixed loop that won't slip, yet unties easily even after being loaded — "the king of knots."
Cleat hitch
Secures a line to a deck cleat with figure-eight wraps finished by a locking hitch — fast to release under load.
Bowline: "the rabbit comes out of the hole, around the tree, and back down the hole"
Example
You're coming into the dock and need to secure a line to a piling loop, plus tie the other end to the boat's deck cleat so it can be released quickly.
1
Use a bowline to form a loop around the piling — it holds firm but won't jam.
2
Use a cleat hitch on the boat end so crew can release it fast if needed.
F

Points of Sail

  • Identify the no-sail zone and every point of sail around the wind clock
The "wind clock": starting from the no-sail zone at top and rotating either direction — close hauled → close reach → beam reach → broad reach → run.
Points of sail, from closest to the wind to furthest
No-sail zone ("in irons")
~45° cone pointed straight into the wind. Sails luff — no power possible here.
Close hauled
As close to the wind as possible while still full and driving. Zig-zagging upwind on this point of sail is called beating.
Close reach
Between close hauled and beam reach.
Beam reach
Wind directly on the side (90°) — usually the fastest, easiest point of sail.
Broad reach
Between beam reach and a run; wind coming from behind at an angle.
Run
Wind directly behind you, sailing straight downwind.
Example
The race mark is directly upwind of you. Why can't you just sail straight at it?
1
A course straight upwind sits inside the no-sail zone, where sails just luff.
2
You have to zig-zag: sail close hauled on one tack, tack through the wind, sail close hauled on the other tack, and repeat — this is beating to the mark.
G

Bernoulli's Principle

  • Explain how a curved sail generates a sideways/forward force from wind
The sail splits the wind. The outside (leeward) curve is longer, so air travels faster there and pressure drops. Air on the inside (windward) curve is slower and pressure stays higher. The pressure difference pushes the sail toward the low-pressure (leeward) side.
Why it matters

A sail is really a vertical airfoil, like an airplane wing turned on its side. The faster/slower airflow on either side creates a pressure difference, and that difference creates a force that drives the sail (and the boat) toward the low-pressure side. This lift-based force is what lets a boat sail upwind and even faster than the wind on some points of sail — pure "wind pushing the sail like a parachute" can't do that.

Example
Why can a well-trimmed sailboat sail faster than the true wind speed on a reach, if the wind is what's powering it?
1
If sails only worked by wind "pushing" them like a parachute, boat speed could never exceed wind speed.
2
Because the sail generates lift (Bernoulli's principle) rather than just drag, it can keep producing forward force even as apparent wind speed and angle change with boat speed — allowing speeds that exceed true wind speed on the right points of sail.
H

Movement of the Boat

  • Yaw, roll, pitch — and what causes each
  • Weather helm and side slippage
  • How to stop the boat, tack, and jibe
Three axes of motion: yaw (rudder/keel), roll (wind on the sail — countered by hiking out), pitch (waves and fore-aft weight — sit near midship).
Boat motion & balance
Yaw
Bow swings left/right — caused by the rudder, motor, keel, etc.
Roll
Boat tips side to side (heeling) — caused by wind pressure on the sail; crew hikes out to balance it.
Pitch
Bow/stern bob up and down — caused by waves and fore-aft weight; sit near midship to minimize it.
Weather helm
The boat's natural tendency to turn its bow into the wind when overpowered. A little is normal (a safety valve); a lot means you should depower.
Side slippage (leeway)
Sideways skid from wind pressure, resisted by the keel/centerboard/daggerboard.
Stopping the boat
Head into the wind or ease sails to the "safety position" — sails luff and lose power.
Tacking: turn the bow through the wind. Prepare crew ("ready about") → push the tiller toward the boom → cross through the no-sail zone (brief loss of power) → trim in on the new tack.
Tacking vs. Jibing
Tacking (coming about)Jibing (gybing)
Bow or stern turns through the wind?BowStern
Tiller directionToward the boomAway from the boom
Power during the turnTemporarily lost (sails luff)Continuous — boom can swing forcefully
Prep step"Ready about" — cross through irons"Prepare to jibe" — sheet in main first
Relative dangerLower — gradual, gentleHigher — uncontrolled boom swing risk; keep heads down
Example
You're on starboard tack (wind hitting your right/starboard side, main out to port) and want to turn so the wind hits your left/port side instead — which way do you push the tiller, and what do you call the maneuver if your bow crosses through the wind?
1
Turning the bow through the wind is tacking. You push the tiller toward the boom.
2
After the tack, the wind now hits your port side — you're on port tack, and by the "port gives way to starboard" rule you'd need to yield to any boat still on starboard tack.
I

The Rules of the Road

  • Stand-on vs. give-way boats
  • Identify who has right of way from a picture
Crossing situation: the boat on port tack must give way to the boat on starboard tack.
Core right-of-way rules
Opposite tacks
Port tack gives way to starboard tack.
Same tack
The windward boat gives way to the leeward boat.
Overtaking
The boat doing the overtaking gives way, regardless of tack.
Power vs. sail
Power generally gives way to sail (exceptions: narrow channels, fishing vessels, vessels restricted in ability to maneuver).

Stand-on boat: has the right of way — must hold course and speed. Give-way boat: must take early, obvious action (turn or slow) to avoid the stand-on boat.

Example — identify from a picture
Two sailboats are converging. Boat A has the wind on its starboard side with the boom out to port. Boat B has the wind on its port side with the boom out to starboard. Who stands on?
1
Boat A: wind on starboard side = starboard tack.
2
Boat B: wind on port side = port tack.
Boat A (starboard tack) is the stand-on vessel; Boat B (port tack) must give way.
J

Mooring and Docking

  • Draw a simple boat with correct sail orientation
  • Explain why you approach from leeward
Approach a dock or mooring heading up into the wind from leeward — the sails luff and lose power naturally, giving you a slow, controlled stop right at the target.
Why leeward, not windward

Approaching from leeward means you're heading toward the wind as you arrive — your sails luff, power bleeds off, and the boat naturally slows to a stop. Approaching from windward means the wind is pushing you toward the dock the whole way in, with no natural way to slow down — a recipe for a hard, uncontrolled arrival.

Example
Sketch a simple top-down boat approaching a mooring ball with "proper sail orientation."
1
Draw the wind arrow pointing at the mooring ball.
2
Draw the boat's final approach heading directly into that wind arrow (bow pointed at the wind/mooring).
3
Draw the sail luffing (flapping, not full/curved) since the boat is head-to-wind and generating no power.
K

Understanding the Wind

  • True wind vs. apparent wind
  • The slot effect and reefing
Apparent wind = true wind + the "wind" created by your own boat speed, added as vectors. It shifts forward (and often strengthens) the faster you go.
Key ideas
True wind
What you'd feel standing still, e.g. on the dock.
Apparent wind
What you actually feel on a moving boat — true wind combined with the wind of your own motion.
Telltales
Read the apparent wind in real time to trim sails correctly (see Section A).
Slot effect
The gap between the jib's leech and the main's luff accelerates air onto the leeward side of the mainsail, boosting its lift.
Reefing
Reducing sail area in strong wind to cut heeling and keep the boat controllable and safe.
Example
True wind is 10 knots. You're sailing a beam reach at 6 knots. Will the wind on your face feel like it's coming from further forward or further aft than the true wind direction, and will it feel stronger or weaker than 10 knots?
1
Adding your own boat-speed "wind" to the true wind always shifts the apparent wind forward.
2
Combining the two vectors also makes the apparent wind stronger than the true wind alone.
It will feel like it's coming from further forward, and it will feel stronger than 10 knots.
L

Crew Overboard (COB)

  • Sketch a recovery path
  • Approach from leeward
A figure-eight recovery path: sail away, loop back, and make the final approach from leeward so the boat luffs to a stop right beside the victim.
Immediate steps
1. Shout & point
Yell "Crew overboard!" and have one person point continuously at the victim.
2. Throw flotation
Toss a cushion or throwable device toward the victim.
3. Recovery maneuver
Sail a figure-eight (or quick-stop) path back to the victim.
4. Approach from leeward
Head up into the wind on the final approach so the boat slows and stops beside them — same logic as docking.
Example
Why not approach the person in the water from windward (with the wind at your back)?
1
Approaching from windward means the wind keeps pushing your hull toward them with no easy way to stop, risking a collision.
2
Approaching from leeward lets you head into the wind, luff the sails, and glide to a controlled stop right next to them — the same reasoning as a dock or mooring approach.
M

Anchoring

  • Understand scope and how to set an anchor
Scope = ratio of rode (anchor line) length to total depth (water depth + freeboard). A low angle at the bottom lets the anchor dig in and hold.
Anchoring basics

Pick a sheltered spot with good holding bottom (sand/mud hold better than rock or grass). Let out enough scope — typically a 5:1 to 7:1 ratio of rode length to total depth (water depth + freeboard) — so the anchor pulls at a shallow angle and digs in instead of just skipping along the bottom. Back down gently under power or sail to set it, then check nearby landmarks to confirm you aren't dragging.

Example
You're anchoring in 10 feet of water; your bow sits 3 feet above the waterline. Using 6:1 scope, how much rode should you let out?
1
Total depth = water depth + freeboard = 10 + 3 = 13 ft.
2
Rode length = scope ratio × total depth = 6 × 13.
Let out about 78 feet of rode.
N

Weather

  • Recognize storm warning signs
  • Prevent heat illness and hypothermia
A tall, anvil-shaped, darkening cloud is your signal to head to shore before the storm arrives.
Weather & health hazards
Cumulonimbus clouds
Tall, anvil-shaped storm clouds — warn of thunderstorms, lightning, and sudden gusts.
Sunstroke & hydration
Open water reflects extra UV; drink water regularly and watch for headache, dizziness, or hot/dry skin.
Hypothermia
Dangerous drop in core body temperature — cold water/wind on wet skin is the biggest risk, even in "warm" weather. Watch for shivering and confusion.
Example
Mid-afternoon on a warm day, you notice a tall cloud on the horizon with a flat, spreading top and a dark, ominous base. What should you do?
1
That description matches a cumulonimbus cloud — a thunderstorm cell.
2
Head to shore immediately rather than waiting to see if it arrives — lightning and sudden gusts can hit before the storm visibly reaches you.
O

PFDs (Personal Flotation Devices)

  • Explain what a PFD provides
  • Check a PFD for proper fit
Fit check: grab the shoulder straps and lift — a properly fitted PFD stays put instead of riding up.
What a PFD does
Buoyancy
Keeps you afloat with minimal effort.
Warmth
Adds insulation and keeps more of your body out of cold water, slowing hypothermia.
Proper fit
Snug enough that it won't ride up over your chin or ears when you raise your arms; always zip/buckle it fully before getting underway.
Example
Before leaving the dock, how do you check that your PFD actually fits?
1
Zip/buckle it snugly, then have someone grab the shoulder straps and lift straight up.
2
If it rides up past your ears or chin, it's too loose — tighten the side straps and re-test.
